                    SCAM AWARENESS

                    Frequently asked questions

                    The warning signs consumer-protection agencies teach, and how VIGIL checks them automatically before you act.

                    What are the most common warning signs of a scam? ▾

                    Consumer-protection agencies such as the U.S. FTC and Australia's Scamwatch point to the same core red flags:

                    • Urgency and pressure — “act now,” “your account will be blocked today.” Real organizations give you time to verify.
                    • Unexpected contact claiming a problem or a prize — suspicious activity, a refund you're owed, or winnings from a draw you never entered.
                    • Requests for money or personal information — OTPs, passwords, card numbers, or payment by gift card, wire transfer, or crypto.
                    • Links or attachments you didn't ask for — especially shortened or look-alike addresses (paypa1.com, bit.ly).
                    • Requests for secrecy — “don't tell the bank” or “keep this between us” is a hallmark of fraud.
                    • Too good to be true — guaranteed returns, free money, or a deal that expires in minutes.

                    One sign alone can be an honest mistake. Several together are the usual shape of a scam — which is why VIGIL correlates evidence instead of judging any single signal.

                    How do I spot a phishing message or email? ▾

                    Look past the branding — logos are copied in seconds:

                    • Check the sender — display names are trivially spoofed; the real address behind the name often reveals a stranger's domain.
                    • Watch the story — phishing messages follow a script: a problem with your account, a payment that failed, a login you don't recognize, an invoice you never made.
                    • Treat credential asks as hostile — no legitimate service needs your password or OTP from a message you didn't initiate.
                    • Beware greetings like “Dear customer” — your bank knows your name.
                    Is it safe to click the link in this message? ▾

                    Check the destination before you touch it:

                    • Hover or long-press the link to preview the real address — it matters more than the visible text.
                    • Shorteners hide everything — treat bit.ly-style links in unexpected messages as untrusted.
                    • Look-alike domains swap letters for digits (rn for m, 1 for l) or append the real brand to an unrelated domain.
                    • If a “bank” links you to a login page, don't go through the link — open the site yourself by typing the address you already use.

                    VIGIL inspects the address as text and will not open it, so you can review a link here without exposing your browser to it.

                    Why would a scammer send a QR code? ▾

                    A QR code hides its destination, so you can't read where it goes before scanning — a trick now called “quishing.” Common plays:

                    • Swapped payment stickers — a genuine parking-meter or merchant QR replaced with one paying the scammer.
                    • Login pages in image form — a code that opens a fake sign-in page asking for your credentials.
                    • Payment requests in messages — UPI or wallet QRs attached to “refund” or “verification” stories.

                    VIGIL Vision decodes QR codes locally and analyzes the destination text without ever opening it.

                    What should I do if I already replied or shared information? ▾

                    Act quickly — most damage happens in the first hour:

                    • Shared a password? Change it now, starting with your email and banking accounts.
                    • Shared an OTP or card details? Call your bank on the official number from their website or your card — never a number from the message.
                    • Paid by gift card, wire, or crypto? Contact the company immediately and report it; recovery is rare but faster reports help.
                    • Report it — reportfraud.ftc.gov (US), scamwatch.gov.au (AU), or your local cybercrime portal; forward phishing texts to 7726 (SPAM).
                    How does VIGIL decide whether something is a scam? ▾

                    VIGIL matches content against known scam patterns — credential and payment requests, urgency, impersonation, suspicious destinations, and hidden instructions aimed at AI agents — then correlates what it finds, so no single weak signal can produce a verdict on its own:

                    • ALLOW — no known signals matched. This is never a guarantee that content is safe.
                    • WARN — some signals matched; pause and verify before acting.
                    • DENY — high-risk rules matched. Rules decide denial; the optional local model can add a warning but never clear one.

                    Everything runs on this device — VIGIL never visits the links you submit and sends nothing to a cloud service.
